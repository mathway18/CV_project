\begin{abstract}
Temporal consistency is the central bottleneck of practical video object removal: frame-wise plausible results often still exhibit flicker, boundary instability, or drift over time. We study a three-stage course pipeline: (1) a hand-crafted baseline with detection, motion filtering, and classical inpainting; (2) an AI-driven pipeline combining SAM 2 and ProPainter; and (3) a quality-gated extension with keyframe repair and frame-level fallback. We evaluate mandatory datasets (wild corridor, \textit{bmx-trees}, \textit{tennis}) using no-reference boundary seam and temporal warp metrics for Part 2 vs. Part 3. Experiments show that quality-gated refinement improves seam and temporal consistency on \textit{bmx-trees} and \textit{tennis}, while preserving an already strong corridor baseline by rejecting harmful edits. This indicates adaptive acceptance is more reliable than unconditional refinement in real-world restoration. GitHub repository: \url{https://github.com/mathway18/CV_project}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Removing dynamic objects from videos and restoring plausible clean backgrounds is a representative problem in computer vision with applications in post-production, robotics, autonomous perception, and visual content editing. The problem is challenging because high-quality results require both accurate object localization over time and temporally coherent filling of large occluded regions.

Our project follows a progressive roadmap. In Part 1, we implement a hand-crafted baseline using YOLO-based segmentation, Lucas--Kanade optical flow filtering, mask dilation, and temporal-first restoration with OpenCV inpainting fallback. In Part 2, we reproduce a modern AI-driven strategy: SAM 2 for dynamic masks and ProPainter for video inpainting. In Part 3, we extend the system with quality-gated keyframe refinement, where candidate repaired frames are accepted only if local no-reference checks indicate improvement.

Our key contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A complete end-to-end implementation spanning traditional and modern pipelines for video object removal.
    \item A practical quality-gated refinement mechanism that avoids over-editing and preserves stable outputs.
    \item Quantitative and qualitative analysis across all mandatory datasets, including accepted/rejected refinement statistics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Object Detection and Segmentation Foundations}
R-CNN~\cite{girshick2014rcnn} and Mask R-CNN~\cite{he2017maskrcnn} established two-stage detection/instance segmentation, while YOLO~\cite{redmon2016yolo} provided a strong real-time one-stage alternative. DETR~\cite{carion2020detr} introduced transformer-based end-to-end detection. Segment Anything (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} generalized promptable segmentation, and SAM 2~\cite{ravi2024sam2} extended this capability to video settings.

\subsection{Video Object Tracking and Dynamic Segmentation}
Track-Anything~\cite{yang2023trackanything} combines SAM-style segmentation with temporal tracking for interactive video object extraction. Recent geometry-centric methods such as VGGT4D~\cite{hu2025vggt4d}, built on visual geometry transformers~\cite{wang2025vggt}, show another route to dynamic cue mining. We treat newer foundation-model directions (SAM 3~\cite{carion2025sam3}, MapAnything~\cite{keetha2025mapanything}, Pi3~\cite{wang2025pi3}) as latest/concurrent frontiers rather than mature baselines, and reference them to position our work relative to rapidly evolving research trends.

\subsection{Video Inpainting and Generative Restoration}
Classical flow-guided completion FGVC~\cite{gao2020fgvc} and end-to-end E2FGVI~\cite{li2022e2fgvi} advanced temporal inpainting quality. ProPainter~\cite{zhou2023propainter} combines dual-domain propagation and sparse transformers to handle large occlusions robustly and serves as our Part 2 core inpainting engine. For hard cases where background content never appears in the sequence, latent diffusion~\cite{rombach2022ldm} with controllable conditioning (e.g., ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023controlnet}) provides a promising generative fallback.

\subsection{Related Work Summary and Gap}
Prior systems substantially improve segmentation and inpainting quality, but they are still commonly deployed as \emph{always-on} refinement pipelines. In our mandatory datasets, this assumption is fragile: some sequences benefit from additional edits, while already-clean sequences can degrade under unnecessary post-processing. This gap motivates our Part 3 design: a quality gate that turns refinement into a selective decision process, retaining ProPainter strengths while reducing regression risk.

\subsection{Part 1: Hand-crafted Baseline}
\textbf{Dynamic mask extraction.}
We detect target categories and estimate object-wise motion magnitude using sparse optical flow on internal feature points. Static instances are filtered out, and binary masks are dilated to cover object boundaries and motion blur.

\textbf{Background restoration.}
For each masked pixel, we prioritize temporal borrowing from neighboring frames at identical spatial locations, then apply classical inpainting when temporal evidence is unavailable. This design is lightweight and easy to reproduce, but tends to blur highly textured or large missing regions.

\subsection{Part 2: AI-driven Pipeline}
\textbf{SAM 2 mask refinement.}
We use detector cues as prompts and run SAM 2 for high-quality dynamic segmentation. Practical adjustments include restricting SAM 2 predictions with detector masks and disabling temporal stabilization by default when it propagates false positives.

\textbf{ProPainter inpainting.}
Frame-wise masks are passed to ProPainter, which leverages motion/feature propagation and sparse transformer modeling to produce temporally consistent restored videos.

\subsection{Part 3: Quality-Gated Keyframe Refinement}
Part 3 introduces a conservative refinement strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Select keyframes with non-empty masks.
    \item Generate candidate repairs (OpenCV backend for reproducibility; optional diffusion backend for exploration).
    \item Softly blend repaired content into Part 2 outputs.
    \item Accept the candidate only if local no-reference checks improve seam/temporal behavior and satisfy regression thresholds; otherwise fall back to Part 2.
\end{enumerate}
Unlike unconditional post-processing, this stage is explicitly formulated as a \emph{decision problem}. For each candidate frame $I_t^{\text{P3}}$ and baseline frame $I_t^{\text{P2}}$ (P2/P3 denote Part 2/Part 3 outputs), we evaluate local boundary seam, temporal warp consistency, and masked-region change magnitude. A frame is accepted only when all criteria below are satisfied:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gate}
\mathrm{Accept}_t=\mathbf{1}\!\left[
\Delta S_t \le 0 \;\wedge\;
\Delta W_t \le \tau_w \;\wedge\;
D_t \le \tau_d
\right],
\end{equation}
where $\Delta S_t=S_t^{\text{P3}}-S_t^{\text{P2}}$ is seam change, $\Delta W_t=W_t^{\text{P3}}-W_t^{\text{P2}}$ is temporal-warp change, $D_t$ is masked-region drift, and $(\tau_w,\tau_d)$ are safety thresholds.
If any criterion fails, we directly copy $I_t^{\text{P2}}$ to the final output. The key benefit is dual-regime robustness: Part 3 improves difficult sequences with large texture/occlusion, and it preserves strong Part 2 outputs in easy or already-clean sequences.

\subsection{Quality-Gated Refinement Algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:qgr} presents the core control logic of Part 3. The important point is that refinement is not treated as an unconditional image-to-image transform; it is a candidate generation step followed by a conservative acceptance test.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Quality-Gated Refinement}
\label{alg:qgr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Original video $X_{1:T}$, Part 2 output $P_{1:T}$, masks $M_{1:T}$, stride $s$, thresholds $(\tau_w,\tau_d)$
\ENSURE Final refined video $Y_{1:T}$
\STATE $\mathcal{K} \leftarrow \{t \mid t \bmod s = 0 \;\land\; |M_t| > 0\}$
\FOR{$t=1$ to $T$}
    \IF{$|M_t| = 0$}
        \STATE $Y_t \leftarrow P_t$; \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Find the nearest repaired keyframe in $\mathcal{K}$ or repair frame $t$ directly
    \STATE Generate a candidate $\hat{Y}_t$ with the OpenCV backend (or optional diffusion backend)
    \STATE Soft-blend $\hat{Y}_t$ into $P_t$ inside the dilated mask
    \STATE Compute seam change $\Delta S_t$, temporal change $\Delta W_t$, and masked drift $D_t$
    \IF{$\Delta S_t \le 0$ and $\Delta W_t \le \tau_w$ and $D_t \le \tau_d$}
        \STATE $Y_t \leftarrow \hat{Y}_t$
    \ELSE
        \STATE $Y_t \leftarrow P_t$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $Y_{1:T}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate all mandatory videos: \textit{bmx-trees} (80 frames, $432\times240$), \textit{tennis} (70 frames, $432\times240$), and wild corridor (192 frames, $1280\times720$). Part 2 is the fixed baseline (SAM 2 + ProPainter). Part 3 is the quality-gated keyframe refinement stage built on top of those outputs.

\subsection{Metrics}
Because clean ground-truth videos are unavailable for these mandatory sequences, we use no-reference diagnostics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Boundary seam score} (lower is better): discontinuity around mask boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Temporal warp error} (lower is better): frame-to-frame inconsistency after motion warping.
    \item \textbf{Masked texture statistic} (diagnostic): local high-frequency content in masked regions.
    \item \textbf{Gate acceptance behavior}: Accepted/rejected frames and acceptance rate.
\end{itemize}
These metrics are computed from saved outputs in \texttt{outputs/part3\_*/part3\_eval.json} and summarized in \texttt{outputs/part3\_metrics\_summary.json}.

Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that Part 3 improves both seam and temporal consistency on \textit{bmx-trees} and \textit{tennis}, while preserving wild corridor unchanged. \subsection{Per-Dataset Analysis}
\textit{bmx-trees}. This sequence has challenging high-frequency background content (trees/branches), where seam artifacts are easy to introduce. The gate accepts only 15/80 frames, but this small subset is sufficient to yield measurable gains: seam improves by 1.03\% and temporal warp by 3.86\%. The relatively low acceptance rate indicates that many candidate edits are risky in this scene; selective acceptance prevents quality collapse.

\textit{tennis}. This sequence admits many stable corrections (53/70 accepted). Seam improves by 3.57\% and temporal warp by 0.80\%, showing that accepted edits are mostly safe and useful. The larger texture drop suggests stronger smoothing in masked regions; however, the boundary and temporal metrics still improve, indicating a favorable trade-off for this dataset.

\textit{wild corridor}. All 192 candidate frames are rejected, and Part 3 exactly matches Part 2. This is not a failure; it is intended behavior of quality-gated refinement when the baseline is already strong and additional edits are likely to be harmful. The gate therefore acts as a safety mechanism that protects an already good restoration.

The ablation clarifies the role of the gate.

On \textit{bmx-trees}, unconditional acceptance achieves the lowest seam score, but it does so by aggressively smoothing masked regions. Mean masked texture drops from 13.22 (Part 2) to 10.45, whereas the gated variant retains substantially more structure at 12.85. This matches the frame-level rejection statistics: 57/65 rejected \textit{bmx-trees} candidates were blocked due to texture loss, 7 due to seam regression, and 1 due to temporal regression.

On \textit{tennis}, the candidate generator is already stable, so the gate makes only a small numerical difference. Most rejections there are triggered by temporal regression checks (10 frames) or seam checks (7 frames).

The practical takeaway is that the gate is primarily a \emph{risk controller}. It may sacrifice a small amount of average seam reduction in exchange for better texture preservation and slightly better local temporal stability.

\subsection{Failure Modes and Trade-offs}
The results also expose meaningful trade-offs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No-reference limitation.} Seam and warp are useful diagnostics, but they may not fully capture perceptual realism. This is most apparent in large uniform regions where seam reduction can coincide with over-smoothing.
    \item \textbf{Resolution mismatch.} Datasets have different spatial resolutions (corridor at $1280\times720$ vs. others at $432\times240$). Absolute metric magnitudes are therefore not directly interchangeable across datasets; within-dataset trends are more reliable.
    \item \textbf{Texture vs. smoothness tension.} Masked texture can decrease even when seam/warp improve, so final judgment should combine quantitative diagnostics with direct inspection of rendered outputs.
    \item \textbf{Conservative acceptance.} The quality gate is intentionally asymmetric: it is easier to reject a dubious edit than to accept it. This protects the baseline, but it can also leave some potentially helpful refinements unused.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Discussion}
\subsection{Why Conditional Refinement Matters}
Overall, the experiments support a practical lesson for video restoration: \emph{refinement should be conditional}. A global always-on post-processing step is brittle, while quality gating enables dataset-specific adaptation. In difficult scenes, a small set of accepted edits is enough to improve stability; in already-clean scenes, conservative rejection prevents regressions. This asymmetry is desirable in production-style restoration, where a visible failure is often more costly than a missed marginal improvement.

\subsection{Scene-Level Limitations}
Our system is still limited by the amount of recoverable background evidence in the input video. If an object occludes the same region for too long, or if the camera never reveals the hidden background from any nearby frame, then both the OpenCV backend and the ProPainter baseline are forced to extrapolate from weak context. In those cases the output tends to become over-smooth, repetitive, or structurally ambiguous. The gate can reject harmful edits, but it cannot invent missing world knowledge by itself.

Another limitation is upstream mask quality. Detector misses, SAM leakage, or overly conservative masks directly affect the region that is repaired. Part 3 can prevent some regressions after the fact, but it cannot fully recover when the object mask is badly misaligned or semantically incomplete. This is especially important for thin structures, motion blur, and fast camera motion, where the object boundary itself is uncertain.

\subsection{Generative Fallback Potential}
The repository includes an optional diffusion-based inpainting backend as a fallback for exactly the extreme regime above: when the missing background is never observed and classical borrowing is insufficient. Its main advantage is stronger semantic imagination. In principle, it can restore walls, floor patterns, or vegetation that never appear in neighboring frames.

Its disadvantages are equally important. It is much more expensive in compute and memory, depends on large external weights, and produces less deterministic outputs than the OpenCV path used in our final report. For this reason, we treat diffusion as a promising extension rather than the default solution for the mandatory benchmark.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a complete video object removal project that progresses from classical computer-vision baselines to modern foundation-model-based restoration and an adaptive Part 3 extension. With mandatory datasets, the final system demonstrates two complementary strengths: (1) measurable improvement where refinement is beneficial (\textit{bmx-trees}, \textit{tennis}), and (2) safe non-intervention where the baseline is already near-optimal (wild corridor).
Future work includes larger evaluation on benchmarks with ground truth, stronger video foundation models for dynamic mask quality, and learning-based acceptance policies that replace manual thresholds with data-driven reliability prediction.
